TL;DR

Problem
The Build Trap
Workday had a deep culture of shipping the org chart — executive pressure to show progress fast, without checking if anyone wanted what was being built. The Variance Analysis project spent three years in development. Users didn't want it. Nobody asked first.
Solution
An Incubation Playbook
Co-created with Luis Guzman: a repeatable framework that forces a viability check before build. Teams validate desirability, feasibility, and business value in days, not quarters — and get a clear kill/continue decision.
Outcome
Faster Kills, Faster Bets
The playbook killed low-viability work early (saving 3+ years of engineering cost) and aligned an entire division roadmap in three days at the Payroll Summit.

Key Features

The Viability Gate

Before anyone writes code, an idea has to clear a desirability/feasibility/value gate. Most ideas die here — and that's the point. A fast no is worth more than a slow maybe.

The incubation funnel with the viability gate up front

Three-Day Alignment

The Payroll Summit compressed what's normally a quarter of roadmap debate into three days, because the framework gives everyone the same criteria to argue from.

Payroll Summit roadmap alignment board

Teaching Through the Tool

The playbook is a facilitation toolkit, not a deck. Teams run it themselves — which is how it scaled past me and Luis.

Facilitation toolkit: cards, canvases, decision criteria

Deep Dive

The Kill DecisionWhy killing Variance Analysis was the win

The sunk-cost trap

Three years in, the instinct was to ship something to justify the investment. The playbook reframed it: the cheapest path forward was to stop.

What it freed up

Those engineers moved to work users actually wanted. The 3+ years saved is opportunity cost, not just budget.

ReflectionMaking a no feel like a win

What worked

Giving teams the criteria and the language to kill their own ideas without it feeling like failure.

What I'd refine

Documenting the methodology so it survives without its authors in the room.
