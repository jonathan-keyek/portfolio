Workday

To Break a C-Suite Deadlock
One agentic prototype turned a stalled governance debate into a funded two-year roadmap — and a shipping product.

TL;DR

Problem
The Maintenance Trap
Workday was losing deals to "too expensive," and the fix on the table was to turn more features on by default. Wrong diagnosis. Admins weren't refusing features — they were already drowning in maintenance. Turning more on was pouring water on a drowning person.
Solution
Agentic, with a Glass Box
Agents that take on the maintenance while keeping the admin in command. Every action shown, explained, and reversible — the Glass Box pattern makes the agent's reasoning legible the moment it acts.
Outcome
From Deadlock to Product
A 45-minute prototype walkthrough broke the stalemate, funded six engineering teams, and made Glass Box the company's governance standard. The vision is now shipping as Workday's Deployment Agent.
Process
What I Actually Did

Synthesis in FigJam. Pulled 51 existing research reports and 17 SME sessions into FigJam — affinity-mapped the findings until the Maintenance Trap framing fell out of the data. The diagnosis came from clustering what was already there, not from a fresh research budget.

Planning and flows in Figma. Mapped the admin's reality, the agentic interaction patterns, and the Glass Box approval flow in Figma before any code. This is where supervised autonomy went from a concept to a concrete sequence of states.

Prototyping in Lovable and Claude Code. A static mock can't sell an agent that reasons, proposes, and waits — so I built a live-coded, non-deterministic prototype in Lovable and Claude Code. Real interaction, real reasoning surfaced, real undo. The thing leaders touched in the room was running code, not a deck.

Communicating in Loom. Kept a globally distributed team aligned async — design in Hawaii, engineering in San Francisco, research in Dublin — with Loom walkthroughs of each prototype iteration. Decisions moved across time zones without waiting on a meeting.

Validating at Workday Rising EMEA. Took the live prototype to the floor — laptops for hands-on interaction, structured 45-minute Braindates, booth intercepts. Targeted solo admins without a support bench specifically. That's where the language gap surfaced and every label got rewritten from database-speak ("Business Process Definition") into plain human terms ("3 hiring workflows need attention before your next release").

Key Features

The Glass Box

Trust comes from visibility. Before the agent changes anything, it surfaces what it intends to do, why, and how to stop or undo it — turning opaque automation into a legible, accountable action.

Glass Box — agent intent surfaced with intervention controls

Reversible by Design

Every agentic action carries a clear path back. Admins delegate the tedium without surrendering control, because nothing the agent does is a one-way door.

Reversible action flow — delegate without losing control

The Impact Analyzer

Before any change executes, it simulates the downstream consequences and shows you what would break. It turns a fear-based gamble into a calculated decision.

Impact Analyzer — simulated consequences before execution

Deep Dive

Trust as the Real DeliverableWhy governance, not automation, was the hard part

The blocker was trust, not capability

The technology could automate maintenance long before leadership would let it. The design problem was making the agent's behavior trustworthy enough to delegate to.

Legibility unlocked the budget

Once leaders could see exactly what the agent would do and undo, the risk calculus flipped — and the funding followed.

What Didn't WorkThe directions that didn't survive feasibility

Goal-Based Configuration died on the table

The seductive idea: let admins state a business outcome — "reduce IT tickets 20%" — and have the agent configure the system to get there. It collapsed under technical reality. The gap between that intent and the specific configuration objects required to achieve it was too wide to bridge without the AI guessing. Enterprise software doesn't forgive hallucination. Killing it early saved us from shipping a feature that would've eroded the exact trust we were trying to build.

The filtered view was the wrong kind of simple

Our first instinct for solo admins was to hide the advanced controls — a stripped-down enterprise tool. The POC phase proved that wrong. They don't need less Workday; they need one built for their reality: one person, high stakes, no support bench. We stopped subtracting and started designing for the actual user. That pivot is what made the agentic direction the right one.

The pattern

Both were good ideas that didn't survive contact with feasibility. Cutting them wasn't a setback — it was the work. The version that got funded was sharper precisely because these two didn't make it in.

ReflectionDesigning the argument, not just the feature

What worked

Putting a live prototype in decision-makers' hands beat any amount of slideware. The experience carried the argument.

What I'd refine

The vision touched Platform, Tech Stack, Sales, Brand, and Design at once — breadth sold it, but created downstream scope that was hard to prioritize. Next time: find the 5–6 highest-leverage levers early and go deep. And bring the Glass Box trust framing to leadership from day one — it was the actual unlock, and it deserved the spotlight sooner.
